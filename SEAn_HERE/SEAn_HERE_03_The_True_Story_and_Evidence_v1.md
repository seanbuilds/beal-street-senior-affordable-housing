## Part 1: The Narrative Essay

### The True Story of 100 Beal Street: What Really Happened in Hingham

In the late 1980s, the Town of Hingham faced an important question: where to locate a permanent, supportive home for vulnerable adolescents who needed specialized residential care. In 1989, the Selectmen deeded a 15-acre portion of former Navy depot land off Beal Street, known as School Tract II, to the independent Hingham Housing Authority for $45,751 **[1]**. The deed came with an express condition: the land was to be used for a residential educational facility for troubled youth, and if that use ceased, the Town retained a right of re-entry—provided, however, that this right of re-entry expired thirty years from the date of the deed **[1]**. On a two-acre corner of the site, a 12-bed group home was built and has operated quietly for decades under state youth-services contracts **[2]**.

Behind the adolescent home lay 13 undeveloped acres dotted with abandoned World War II naval ammunition bunkers. In 2001, the Town funded the demolition of the bunkers and soil cleanup, signing a Memorandum of Understanding with the Housing Authority and recording a formal survey plan that divided the property into the developed 2-acre home and an 8.02-acre surplus upland plateau (Lot 2) **[2]**. Recognizing that Hingham had a severe shortage of affordable housing for its aging residents, Annual Town Meeting convened in 2006 and voted by an overwhelming declared two-thirds majority to authorize the Selectmen to amend the 1989 deed restriction so this surplus plateau could be developed for affordable senior housing **[3]**. Yet in a municipal oversight that went unnoticed for years, Town Hall never actually executed or recorded that authorized deed release at the Registry of Deeds **[3]**.

Fast forward to February 2019. The deed’s thirty-year calendar anniversary was approaching on March 7, 2019. Town Counsel warned the Board of Selectmen that if the thirty-year window closed without action, the Town's legal right of re-entry would expire under Massachusetts property law. On February 26, 2019, the Select Board met in Executive Session, returned to Open Session, and voted 3–0 to authorize litigation against the Housing Authority to force them to deed the land back to the Town **[4]**. Facing the threat of an immediate lawsuit from Town Hall, the Housing Authority held a special meeting on June 5, 2019, and voted to approve transferring approximately 19 acres back to the Town **[5]**.

To outside observers, the land dispute seemed settled. But local housing authorities are not Town departments; under Massachusetts General Laws (Chapter 121B), they are independent public corporations created by the Legislature to serve low-income residents. Furthermore, because state public housing funds had built the Beal Street home, state law strictly prohibits any housing authority from alienating public housing property without express written approval from the Commonwealth **[5]**. The Massachusetts Department of Housing and Community Development (DHCD, now EOHLC) intervened, warning that the local transfer vote was unauthorized under state law, and on July 18, 2019 recorded a formal Notice of Statutory Transfer Restriction on title at the Registry of Deeds **[6]**. With the state legally blocking the transfer, the Housing Authority reconvened on September 7, 2021, and voted 4–0 to formally rescind the 2019 transfer motion—confirming that the land stays with the Housing Authority for affordable housing **[7]**.

With its ownership reaffirmed, the Housing Authority moved forward to address the community's acute need for affordable senior apartments. In 2024 and 2025, the Authority declared the 8.6-acre parcel (Lot B) available under state procurement laws and issued a comprehensive 155-page Request for Proposals **[8]**. In August 2025, the Housing Authority Board voted unanimously to select Peabody Properties to develop a modest, 3-story, 68-unit rental residence restricted to seniors aged 62 and older **[8]**. Under the proposed 99-year ground lease, the private developer pays all construction costs and a $500,000 upfront fee to the Authority, while the land remains publicly owned and the entire building reverts to the Housing Authority upon lease expiration **[8]**.

If the senior housing project is moving ahead, why do so many residents believe that "Town Meeting killed the project"? This is the single biggest misconception in town. The project that Town Meeting rejected on April 27, 2026 was NOT 100 Beal Street. It was the **Center for Active Living (CAL)**—a separate $29.93 million municipal daytime senior center that the Select Board proposed building inside Bare Cove Park **[9]**. That municipal project required Town Meeting to approve nearly $30 million in taxpayer debt borrowing, which requires a strict two-thirds supermajority under state finance law. The borrowing vote failed (510 Yes to 470 No) **[9]**. Because both projects involved seniors and both bordered the sensitive Tucker's Swamp watershed **[10]**, community debate blurred the two. But 100 Beal Street requires zero Town borrowing, was never voted down at Town Meeting, and remains an active, permitted public housing initiative **[8, 10]**.
