## Part 1: The Human Document (The Complete Plain-English Narrative)

### The True Story of 100 Beal Street: What Really Happened in Hingham

For over three decades, the parcel of land known as **100 Beal Street (School Tract II)** has stood at the crossroads of military history, municipal disputes, state public housing protections, and a severe local shortage of affordable senior apartments. 

In town discussions and social media commentary, contradictory rumors circulate: some believe the Town of Hingham owns the property; others believe the deed reverted back to Town Hall; some believe the land can only be used for troubled teenagers; and many believe Town Meeting voted down the project in April 2026.

Every one of those rumors is contradicted by the recorded deeds, certified meeting minutes, Town Meeting warrants, and state agency rulings. This narrative tells the full, unspun story of what actually happened, backed step-by-step by the verified public records.

---

### Chapter 1: The 1989 Land Grant & The Adolescent Home
In the late 1980s, the Town of Hingham sought a location for a permanent, supportive residential home for adolescents in state care. On March 7, 1989 (recorded April 21, 1989), the Town conveyed 15.014 acres of former U.S. Navy ammunition depot land—known as School Tract II—to the independent **Hingham Housing Authority (HHA)** for **\$45,751.00** pursuant to a 1987 Town Meeting authorization.

The deed contained a specific use restriction: the land was dedicated for an educational and residential facility for troubled adolescents. Critically, on Page 3 of the deed (Book 09097, Page 160), the Town retained a right of re-entry if that adolescent use ceased, **provided, however, that said right of re-entry terminated thirty (30) years from the date of the deed pursuant to Massachusetts General Laws, Chapter 184A, Section 3**.

Shortly after the conveyance, a 12-bed group home was constructed on a dedicated 2.0-acre corner of the site along Beal Street, funded through the Commonwealth’s Chapter 689 specialized public housing program. For over 35 consecutive years, that adolescent care home has operated continuously under active state contracts with the Department of Children and Families (DCF). Because that facility never ceased operations and was never abandoned, the Town never had legal grounds to trigger a default or claim re-entry during the entire thirty-year statutory window.

---

### Chapter 2: The 2001 Bunker Cleanup & The 8-Acre Plateau
Behind the 2-acre adolescent home lay 13 undeveloped acres dotted with abandoned World War II naval ammunition bunkers and earthen berms. In 2001, the Town funded the demolition of the bunkers and environmental soil remediation. 

To formalize the division of the parcel, Gale Associates, Inc. surveyed the land into two distinct parcels: **Lot 1** (the 2.0-acre operating group home) and **Lot 2** (an 8.02-acre surplus upland plateau), recorded at the Registry of Deeds as **Plan 100 of 2001** (Plan Book 44, Page 412). The Town and the Housing Authority entered into an administrative Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) on June 12, 2001 acknowledging the cleanup and division.

---

### Chapter 3: The 2006 Town Meeting Mandate & The Missing Release
Recognizing that Hingham faced an acute shortage of affordable housing for its aging residents, the 2006 Annual Town Meeting took up **Article 38**. On May 1, 2006, Town Meeting voted by an overwhelming declared **two-thirds supermajority** to authorize the Board of Selectmen to amend the 1989 deed restriction to permit affordable senior housing on the surplus plateau.

However, in a municipal oversight that went unnoticed for nearly two decades, **Town Hall never actually drafted, signed, or recorded that deed release at the Plymouth County Registry of Deeds**. While Town Meeting gave the Selectmen full legal authority to release the restriction, the executive step was never completed, leaving the 1989 adolescent condition technically active on the public land records.

---

### Chapter 4: The 2019 Lawsuit Vote & The 30-Year Expiration
Fast forward to February 2019. The thirty-year calendar anniversary of the deed was fast approaching on March 7, 2019. Under Massachusetts property law (M.G.L. c. 184A, § 3), any reversionary right of entry expires automatically after 30 years unless formally re-recorded.

Realizing that the 30-year window was closing, the Board of Selectmen convened in Executive Session on **February 26, 2019** with Town Real Estate Counsel. Citing taxpayer funds spent on the 2001 bunker demolition, the Select Board returned to Open Session and voted **3–0 to authorize litigation against the Housing Authority** to enforce the 2001 MOU and force the return of the land.

Faced with the threat of an immediate lawsuit from Town Hall, the previous HHA Board of Commissioners held a Special Meeting on June 5, 2019 and voted under duress to approve transferring approximately 19 acres back to the Town.

---

### Chapter 5: The Commonwealth Steps In (State Law Vetoes Town Hall)
To outside observers, it appeared Town Hall had recaptured the land. But local housing authorities are not Town departments; under Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 121B, they are independent public corporations created by the Legislature. 

Furthermore, because state public housing funds (Chapter 689) had originally built the Beal Street youth home, state law strictly prohibits any local housing authority from alienating or conveying public housing land without express written approval from the Commonwealth.

The Massachusetts Department of Housing and Community Development (DHCD, now EOHLC) intervened decisively. Associate Director Amy Stitely warned that the local transfer vote was unauthorized under state law. On **July 18, 2019 at 1:44 PM**, DHCD recorded a formal **Notice of Statutory Transfer Restriction** directly on the title at Book 51379, Page 244 under M.G.L. c. 121B, § 34. This state filing legally clouded the title and legally barred Town Hall from taking the property.

On **September 7, 2021**, under new leadership, the HHA Board of Commissioners voted **4–0 to formally rescind the June 5, 2019 transfer motion**, permanently confirming that 100 Beal Street remains in the ownership of the Housing Authority for affordable housing.

---

### Chapter 6: The Peabody Award & The 99-Year Ground Lease
With its fee ownership reaffirmed, the Housing Authority moved forward to address the community’s acute need for affordable senior housing. In 2024 and 2025, the Authority declared the 8.6-acre surplus parcel (Lot B) available under Massachusetts Uniform Procurement Act (M.G.L. c. 30B, § 16) and issued a comprehensive 155-page Request for Proposals (RFP).

On **August 12, 2025**, the HHA Board voted **4–0** to award developer designation to **Peabody Properties / Affordable Housing Scenario Consultants (AHSC)** to build a modest, 3-story, 68-unit rental residence restricted to seniors aged 62 and older.

Under the proposed 99-year ground lease:
* **Municipal Taxpayer Cost:** Exactly **\$0** in Town borrowing, debt exclusions, or taxpayer funds.
* **Upfront Authority Revenue:** The developer pays all development costs and provides a **\$500,000 upfront lease payment** to the Housing Authority.
* **Permanent Public Ownership:** The Housing Authority never sells the land; the Authority retains underlying fee title, and the entire building reverts to the Authority upon lease expiration.

---

### Chapter 7: Debunking the Myth — What Really Failed at Town Meeting?
The single most pervasive misconception in Hingham is the claim that *"Town Meeting voted down 100 Beal Street on April 27, 2026."*

The official certified record of Town Meeting proves this is factually false:
* **What was rejected:** Warrant **Article 12**, which requested **\$29.93 million in municipal debt borrowing** to build the **Center for Active Living (CAL)**—a town-funded municipal senior center building proposed by the Select Board inside Bare Cove Park. Under Massachusetts finance law (M.G.L. c. 44, § 7), debt borrowing requires a strict 2/3 supermajority. The article received 510 Yes to 470 No (52.0% in favor), failing to achieve 2/3.
* **100 Beal Street was NEVER on the warrant:** 100 Beal Street requires zero municipal borrowing, was never voted on, and was never defeated at Town Meeting. Because both projects involved senior citizens and both bordered the Tucker's Swamp watershed, public discussion conflated them.
